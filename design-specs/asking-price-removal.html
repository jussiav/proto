Asking price removal

The asking price is on its way out of the seller journey. It has now left the decision page — the column and the dashed chart line both shipped in September. What is left is the reason the initiative was wider than that removal: the row of figures still duplicates, in different words and a different design, what the offers page already shows the same seller.

Status: 1 shipped · 1 change left to build August 2026 · updated 7 September

How to read this

Two changes, both on the decision page’s auction insights block — the white card above the offers, holding a row of figures, a chart and the auction dates. Change 2 has shipped and is kept below as a record of what production now does. Change 1 is the one still to build.

No new copy. Change 1 adopts labels the offers page already ships. Nothing here needs a translation round.

One thing this initiative does not settle: the asking price still governs what a seller may counter-offer, while no longer appearing anywhere they can see it. That is its own piece of work, listed at the end.

What already shipped

The A/B test that hid the asking price has concluded, and the result was split. Read this before starting change 1 — half of it is already done.

Promoted — live on the consumer decision page

  • The expected-price column is gone. The row is now two cells, in the same old design.
  • The dashed asking-price line is gone, and the price box at the left end of the chart went with it — they were one chart dataset.
  • The flag and its branches are deleted. The hide is now a plain switch on the component, defaulting to hidden, and the consumer page simply does not turn it on.

So change 2 is finished, and the removal half of change 1 is finished. What is left of change 1 is the part the test never touched: replacing the row itself.

Discarded — the negotiation modal kept everything

The same test had an arm that removed the asking price from the counter-offer field as well: the red warning naming it, and the rule that a counter offer may not exceed it. That arm did not win. Both are back in place for every seller.

This matters here because it leaves an inconsistency the removal created rather than solved: the asking price still decides what a seller may offer, while no longer appearing anywhere they can read it. Written up under Not in this initiative yet.

Two smaller notes for whoever picks up change 1

  • B2B was never in the test and still shows all three columns, with Hintavaraus as the third. See the notice in change 1 — it is the reason the shared component needs an optional third cell.
  • The chart’s hidden vertical axis still reads the asking price when sizing its ticks. Harmless with the axis hidden, but it is a dead reference to clean up while you are in the file.

What changes

  • The row above the chart — two cells since the removal shipped — is replaced by the stats block the offers page shows during a live auction: same markup, same labels, same icons, same frame.
  • It stays in the decision page’s existing white card, in the same place above the chart. The chart and the auction dates below it are untouched, with one exception below.
  • The chart loses its top rule. The new block carries its own frame, so the hairline above the chart lands a few pixels beneath it and reads as a second line. The rule below the chart stays.
  • Nothing about the asking price is left to remove here — the third column went with the promoted arm. This change is now purely the swap.
  • The B2B decision page keeps a third column — see the notice below. The B2B offers page shows neither block, so nothing there is affected.
  • The values are the same two numbers the row shows today — offers on this request, and how many dealerships they came from.
  • Two refinements to the block itself, which therefore also change the offers page: the icon moves to the left of the value rather than being pushed to the right edge, and the value is bold. Reading a figure should not mean crossing the cell to find it.

One thing to decide up front

The block is not a component today — it is markup inside the offers page’s auction timer card, which also draws the progress bar, the end date and the registration badge.

Extract the two-column block into a shared component and have both pages use it. Copying the markup across would work today and drift tomorrow, which is the thing this change exists to stop.

The prototype has done exactly that, so the shape is settled before anyone opens the production file: AuctionStats in the component gallery, with every prop and every state it has to cover, mounted by the offers page and the decision page from one bundle. AuctionInsights renders it in place of its own row.

The third column stays for B2B — do not remove it

The B2B seller’s decision page shows the same three-column row, and its third column is the reserve price — labelled Hintavaraus, carrying the same stored value the consumer column does. It is already parameterised: the B2B page passes the label in, which is the only difference between the two.

So this change is consumer-only. A reserve price is a live B2B feature — it binds the seller when a bid meets it — not a legacy field being sunset, and the flag that hides the consumer column was never applied to B2B. Whoever removes it must leave the B2B column where it is.

The shared component keeps that cell available: pass a price and a third cell appears with its own icon; omit it and the row is two cells. Previewed in the gallery, since the prototype has no B2B seller. Without it, one component could not serve both pages and the duplication this change exists to remove would simply move.

Acceptance criteria

  • The decision page and the offers page render the same component for these two figures — not two copies of the same markup. See AuctionStats for the props and states it has to cover.
  • The values are unchanged: offers on the request, and the number of dealerships behind them.
  • No asking price, expected price or price estimate appears anywhere in the insights block — still true after the swap, as it is today.
  • The chart, the auction dates and the surrounding white card are otherwise untouched — only its top rule goes.
  • There is one hairline between the stats block and the chart, not two.
  • It reads correctly at 360px — both labels fit their column without clipping.

Why: The same seller sees both pages within a day, and today they describe the same two numbers twice over, in different words, with different weight and different framing. Reusing the block settles that for free — and the column that has to go for the asking price to leave the page is one of the three it replaces, so the removal costs nothing extra.

2 — The chart drops the dashed asking-price line (shipped)

Shipped · September 2026Decision page · chartAuction ended, offers seen
Nothing to build. This is what production draws today, kept so the change is on the record and so nobody reads the old chart in a screenshot as current. It arrived by promoting the A/B arm rather than as a ticket off this page.

What changed

  • The dashed horizontal line across the chart is gone, and so is the price label in the box at its left end — they were one dataset, so removing it took both.
  • Everything else stayed: the rising bid line, the shaded fill beneath it, the highest-bid label at the right end, and the start and end dates below.
  • The chart now scales to the bids alone. One leftover: the hidden vertical axis still reads the asking price when sizing its ticks — a dead reference worth deleting.

See it in the prototype — both variants now draw one line, because this is production behaviour rather than a proposal

Acceptance criteria

  • The chart draws one line, not two, in every auction state that renders it. — met
  • No price box appears at the left edge; the highest-bid box at the right edge is unchanged. — met
  • The bid line fills the chart vertically off its own highest value. — met; the dead axis reference above is the only tidy-up left
  • B2B still draws both lines, and should. — met; the switch is off for consumers only

Why: The line invited the seller to read the auction as a race against their own asking price — a number the product is removing, and one that was never a prediction of what dealerships would pay. With it gone the chart shows what actually happened.

Not in this initiative yet

The asking price appears elsewhere in the seller journey. These are separate pieces of work, listed so nobody assumes they came with the two changes above — and so the first one is not quietly folded into a neighbouring initiative.

The counter-offer ceiling Own work · will be A/B tested

A seller can be stopped by a number they were never shown. The asking price left the decision page, but it still decides what they may counter-offer. Two places, both live for every seller:

  • A red line under the counter-offer field the moment the typed amount goes above it — “Pyyntihintasi oli [amount]”, which is the first and only time that figure appears.
  • A server-side rule that rejects the counter offer outright: “Vastatarjouksesi ei voi olla asettamaasi pyyntihintaa korkeampi.” It fires only while the highest offer is still below the asking price, and only for a reviewed listing.

Both are settled production behaviour, not an oversight: the arm of the concluded test that removed them was the arm that lost.

What the ceiling actually is, today. It is the stored asking price — or the GT-X estimate where a listing was published without one, because the routing writes the estimate into that field when it auto-publishes. And the field itself can be set from several different sources. So “the asking price” is one name for more than one number, which is part of what makes it hard to explain to a seller.

Do not build this off this page, and do not fold it into Enhanced negotiations. That initiative deliberately leaves the ceiling alone. Resolving it — whether to show the figure, rename it, raise it or drop it — is its own decision and will be settled by an A/B test of its own.
