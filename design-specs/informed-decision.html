Informed decision

A seller with a good offer in hand still hesitates, and the reason is almost always the same: they saw a similar car advertised for thousands more. Two blocks on the decision page answer that, and nothing else — one reads the auction result back as an argument, the other explains what an advertised price is made of.

Read this before anything else

  • The Finnish is placeholder. It is written to make the idea reviewable, not to be shipped, and the team owns the wording once the shape is agreed.
  • This page stays deliberately thin while the idea moves. It describes where the initiative is now, not how it got here, and it gains acceptance criteria and a copy table once the shape is locked.
  • No figures anywhere, on the page or on screen. The second block explains how a price is built, not what the parts cost — see its own section for why that is the design rather than a gap.

Who sees it

Both blocks share one condition, so they can never appear apart. A seller sees them when the auction has ended and a decision is still open — not on an accepted, rejected or expired auction — and the offer clears the team’s fair-offer bar.

Everyone else gets production’s block, untouched, chart and dates included. Never a stripped-down version. A thin auction — fewer than four bidding dealerships — lands there too, because the first block argues from the number of dealerships that competed and that argument does not survive being true of three.

Narrowing to fair offers is a focus decision for this tertial, not a design argument. Both blocks make claims that are only honest for a seller whose offer is already good. Widening the audience later is a deliberate next step and the prototype is built for it: one predicate, read in one place.

A note on the fair-offer flag

The definition of a fair offer is a separate discussion and is not repeated here. The front end already identifies one in production, so this asks for a boolean on the request rather than a threshold the page computes. The prototype does the same — its scenarios carry the flag and nothing in it calculates one.

1 — The auction result, as an answer

Today the block reports two figures and draws a chart of the bidding. The seller’s question is “is this a good offer?”, and two bare numbers answer that only for a reader who already knows how the auction works. The chart is the clearest case: it draws the bids rising, which looks like evidence and is really just the shape every ascending auction makes.

So the chart and the date row go, the figures stay, and the block reads them back as the argument they are. The verdict opens it and the figures close it — evidence reads better after the claim.

2 — Näin vertaat tarjoustasi

The single largest reason a seller rejects, and almost always the same mistake: the number they saw online is a dealership’s advertised price. Nobody was paid that for that car.

So the block states it as an addition the reader completes themselves: the seller’s own offer, plus the dealership’s costs, equals the advertised price. One figure and nothing else — no percentages, no total, no example car.

Only the first term is filled, because it is the only one we hold. The offer is ours to state. The resale price and the costs are not, so they are outlines rather than blocks.

The costs term is dashed, and that is the whole safety mechanism. An equation cannot hide its terms, so this one says the quiet part instead: the box is dashed and its sub-label reads “Vaihtelee autoittain” A dashed box reads as a blank to be filled, not as a quantity — so there is nothing to subtract from, and no proportion is claimed. Filling that chip, or giving it a figure, breaks the block.

Decisions worth keeping

  • The seller’s own figure leads, and the costs are added to it. A diagram that takes costs out of an advertised price says “they took this from you”; one that starts from the seller’s figure and adds says “they added this to yours”. Same facts, accurate direction, none of the resentment.
  • The costs end never carries a figure. A euro amount there turns what a dealership adds into what they take off your price, which is the reaction the block exists to avoid — and it restores the arithmetic the fade removes. A seller told what a margin costs has been handed something to resent rather than something to understand.
  • The figure is the offer as it stands, not the auction result. A negotiation raises the standing amount and the offer card already follows it; the bar reads the same single definition, so it cannot quote a stale price beside a live one. Never below the auction result — a dealership cannot reply lower, and closing a negotiation preserves the amount.
  • No alternative route is named. Not a private sale, not a trade-in, not another dealership. The block answers why that number was bigger and stops. Naming a route we would rather the seller did not take would undo the point of the whole arm.
  • Contrast is measured against the rendered pixels, not an eyeballed colour pair, which says nothing over a gradient: 14.63:1 for the figure and 6.89:1 for the two muted lines, against the 4.5:1 WCAG 2.0 AA threshold that applies at these sizes.

3 — The warm-up screen goes

Production stands a full screen between the seller and their offers the first time they open the decision page. It says the auction went well, and it carries none of the evidence for that.

Block 1 now makes that claim on the page itself, with the figures behind it — same illustration, same message. Keeping both would make the seller read the same sentence twice, once behind a click, and only the second one earns it. So this arm skips the screen and takes the seller straight to the offers.

It reaches first-time visitors only, which is the one state production uses it for — a seller returning to the page has never seen it since. Nothing else changes: the screen is untouched for everyone outside the arm, and it still appears for the auction endings the two blocks do not cover.

Worth deciding together

The screen is the one moment the seller is given nothing to do but read. Removing it trades that for a page that says more and says it in context — but it also removes a deliberate pause before a decision, and an A/B will not separate the two blocks from the missing screen unless we want it to. Worth agreeing whether the removal travels with this arm or gets tested on its own.

Open questions

  1. Does the seller believe the stack without numbers? It is the central bet. The alternative is an illustrative example car, which buys credibility and costs the two reactions this design avoids: arguing with the arithmetic, and resenting a margin. Worth putting in front of the team before the copy is locked.
  2. Does the verdict need the offer as well as the bidder count? The claim fires on a fair offer and a competitive auction. Both are necessary; whether they are sufficient is a question for whoever owns the fair-offer definition.
  3. Does a neutral version outperform this one? Both blocks argue for accepting and sit directly above the reject control. A neutral-framing arm is the obvious partner.
  4. What do the other sellers get? Widening past fair offers is the deliberate next step, and the seller with a weaker offer is the one likeliest to reject. Nothing here is designed for them yet.
