Informed decision

A seller with a good offer in hand still hesitates, and the reason is almost always the same: they saw a similar car advertised for thousands more. Two blocks answer that, and nothing else. Accepting, negotiating, rejecting and walking away — and the page gives them nothing to weigh that choice against. This is a first sketch of a section that does: not numbers, but the trade-offs between the routes a seller actually has.

Who sees it

Both blocks share one condition, so they can never appear apart. A seller sees them when the auction has ended and a decision is still open — not on an accepted, rejected or expired auction — and the offer clears the team’s fair-offer bar.

Everyone else gets production’s block, untouched, chart and dates included. Never a stripped-down version. A thin auction — fewer than four bidding dealerships — lands there too, because the first block argues from the number of dealerships that competed and that argument does not survive being true of three.

Narrowing to fair offers is a focus decision for this tertial, not a design argument. Both blocks make claims that are only honest for a seller whose offer is already good. Widening the audience later is a deliberate next step and the prototype is built for it: one predicate, read in one place.

A note on the fair-offer flag

The definition of a fair offer is a separate discussion and is not repeated here. The front end already identifies one in production, so this asks for a boolean on the request rather than a threshold the page computes. The prototype does the same — its scenarios carry the flag and nothing in it calculates one.

1 — The auction result, as an answer

Today the block reports two figures and draws a chart of the bidding. The seller’s question is “is this a good offer?”, and two bare numbers answer that only for a reader who already knows how the auction works. The chart is the clearest case: it draws the bids rising, which looks like evidence and is really just the shape every ascending auction makes.

So the chart and the date row go, the figures stay, and the block reads them back as the argument they are. The verdict opens it and the figures close it — evidence reads better after the claim.

Three things, each doing one job

  • The verdict states a view and recommends an action. The only place on the decision page that does either. It names no euro figure — it points at the offer card above — so it cannot go stale against the number beside it and it works unchanged on a final offer.
  • The body is the mechanic, and it is the part that does the work. Two claims: the bidders are professionals who looked at the listing, and they bid against each other. Without them, a big number is just a big number to a seller who distrusts the offer.
  • The two figures are different numbers, and that is the point of the row. Bids and bidders. When both read 25 the pair looked like one number printed twice and neither label had to be understood.

Presentation borrows the warm-up screen — illustration, headline, one paragraph. Which is also why this arm removes that screen: production stands a full page in front of a first-time visitor saying much the same thing with none of the evidence, and keeping both makes the seller read the claim twice, once behind a click.

2 — “But I saw similar cars selling for thousands more”

The single largest reason a seller rejects, and almost always the same mistake: the number they saw is a dealership’s advertised price. Nobody was paid that for that car.

It is an equation, and it has to read in one look. Two named prices are the heroes — what the seller was paid, and what the dealership asks — and between them sit four chips for the things a seller forgot were in the second number. No euros, no percentages, no example car. The bar widths are a schematic with no axis and nothing to read a number off.

An earlier version stated the point in two paragraphs around a list of rows. It read as a FAQ, and the prose was doing work the picture should do. If a revision needs a sentence to explain the diagram, the diagram is wrong.

EN — What the seller was paid + margin · warranty · reconditioning · sales costs = the dealership’s asking price. No sentence explains it and none is present — the bars carry the argument, and the lower one is longer by exactly the room the chips take up.

Three decisions worth keeping

  • It is built upwards, and that is the whole design. The seller’s price comes first and everything else is added to reach the advertised figure. A diagram that subtracts from the sticker price says “they took this from you”; one that adds to the seller’s price says “they added this to yours”. Same facts, accurate direction, none of the resentment.
  • The chips are single words, not descriptions. The point is that there are several things a seller forgot about, not what each one covers. A phrase turns a chip into a sentence and the diagram back into a list. A seller told what a margin costs has been handed something to resent rather than something to understand.
  • No alternative route is named. Not a private sale, not a trade-in, not another dealership. The block answers why that number was bigger and stops. Naming a route we would rather the seller did not take would undo the point of the whole arm.

Open questions

  1. Does the seller believe the stack without numbers? It is the central bet. The alternative is an illustrative example car, which buys credibility and costs the two reactions this design avoids: arguing with the arithmetic, and resenting a margin. Worth putting in front of the team before the copy is locked.
  2. Does the verdict need the offer as well as the bidder count? The claim fires on a fair offer and a competitive auction. Both are necessary; whether they are sufficient is a question for whoever owns the fair-offer definition.
  3. Does a neutral version outperform this one? Both blocks argue for accepting and sit directly above the reject control. A neutral-framing arm is the obvious partner.
  4. What do the other sellers get? Widening past fair offers is the deliberate next step, and the seller with a weaker offer is the one likeliest to reject. Nothing here is designed for them yet.
