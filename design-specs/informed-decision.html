Part 1 · for the team

Summary

What it is

  • When an auction ends, some sellers compare their offer with prices on Nettiauto. Those are dealerships’ asking prices, which include the dealership’s costs, margin and room for haggling. They are not what a seller was paid for a car.
  • Variant 6 explains the difference at the moment of decision: a section on the decision page with an example calculation, and a short note under the warm-up card a first-time visitor sees.
  • It is a VWO front-end A/B test: production against variant 6. Nothing in the codebase changes.
  • Shown to every seller who can still reject the auction, whatever their offer. There is no fair-offer condition.

Variant 6 in the prototype · its warm-up · production

Hypothesis

Explaining the difference between a Nettiauto asking price and an AutoVex offer, where the seller decides, reduces the disappointment that comes from comparing the two, and so increases offer acceptance.

Antithesis

Naming Nettiauto may send sellers who would not have compared to go and compare, which could lower acceptance.

Background

A first version of this test ran in VWO in September 2026, as light-blue info boxes inside the auction-details card. It was stopped early because its results could not yet be measured reliably. This version keeps that test’s content and gives it the decision page’s own styling: a section of its own, like the other sections on the page.

The design

  • Decision page: a section of its own directly after the auction-details section, with production’s section heading (icon ph-bold-info) over a white card, the same family as the auction details, Similar cars and Seller protection blocks. Because it is its own section, it works beside either version of the auction-details card.
  • Card: from 768px two equal halves, as the Seller protection card: the text and two check rows on the left, top-aligned; the example on a slate panel on the right. Below 768px the halves stack, text first.
  • Example: an illustrative asking price of 15 000 € as a bar: the purchase part in blue, costs, margin and room for haggling in grey. Blue, not the page’s accept green, because the figures are not the seller’s money; the footnote says they are not about the seller’s car.
  • Warm-up screen: a note card under the “Hyvä tarjouskilpailu takana!” card, and the card’s message drops koko to give the note room. Below 768px the card drops its illustration, so the card and the note fit on one 375×667 screen.
  • Copy follows the C2B terminology table: an offer is never called hinta, and a dealership’s listing price is pyyntihinta, which says it is open to negotiation rather than a final sale price.

Part 2 · build brief

Build a VWO front-end A/B test

Everything needed to build the test is in this part. Build it in VWO, not in the codebase: the build code in B5 is the variation’s whole JavaScript and the source of truth. Use it as written. The previews in part 1 and the prototype run exactly this code. Part 3 of this page is not part of this test.

B1 — What to build

  • One VWO front-end A/B test, two arms. Control: the page as it is. Variant 6: the build code in B5, which makes the two changes below. Name the variation “Variant 6” so results map to this page.
  • Split: this brief assumes an even split between the two arms; see the open question in part 1.
  • Change 1, decision page: a new section after the auction-details section (B2, B5).
  • Change 2, warm-up screen: a note under the warm-up card, the card’s message shortened, and below 768px the card’s illustration hidden (B2, B5).
  • No backend, Vue or stylesheet changes in the codebase. Nothing is stored. No fair-offer or price condition: the variant never reads the seller’s offer.
  • One page: the consumer seller’s decision page. Finnish only, like the page: the code finds its places by Finnish labels.
  • Audience: every visitor of that page. The variant acts only when the hooks in B2 match, so a B2B seller on the same URL sees nothing.
  • Variants 1–5 in part 3 are not part of this test.

B2 — Where it goes and when

  • URL: https://autovex.fi/decision/<tender request id> (route user.offers.decision; logged-in sellers). Target /decision/*.
  • The page is a single-page app. It mounts after load, swaps the warm-up for the offers when the seller presses “Katso tulokset”, and re-renders after every action. Watch the DOM (a MutationObserver on body), keep both elements in step, never insert twice, and remove an element when its condition stops holding.
  • Consumer seller only: act only when at least one consumer offer card is on the page, found by its distance row (“15 km (Helsinki)”): div.rounded-b-lg div.flex.text-xs.text-left.mb-4. The B2B decision page has the same auction-details section and red button but no such row. The warm-up is consumer-only already.
  • Change 1, when: a <button> whose trimmed text is exactly “Hylkää tarjouskilpailu” is on the page. Production renders it while a decision is open and hides it after accepting or rejecting, with no or expired offers, and while the seller waits for a dealership’s reply to a counter offer. Do not read the auction or offer status; this one rule is the condition.
  • Change 1, where: as the next sibling of the auction-details <section>: the <section> whose <h2> text is “Tarjouskilpailun tiedot”, or “Tarjouskilpailun tulos” when the auction-result test has renamed it. The page grid puts 48px between sections.
  • Change 2, when and where: the warm-up card whose <img> src contains excellent_auction (production’s “Hyvä tarjouskilpailu takana!” card; the other warm-up cards use other images). Insert the note as the next sibling of that card, inside the warm-up wrapper; it goes away with the wrapper when the seller presses “Katso tulokset”. The code also marks the image’s parent <div> (av-nt6-media) and the card’s message <p> (av-nt6-msg) and replaces that message (B4).

B4 — Behaviour

  • Decision page section: inserted while the B2 condition holds, removed when it stops (for example after the seller accepts, rejects or sends a counter offer), and put back when it holds again (for example when the dealership replies). Static: it reads nothing from the page or the store.
  • Warm-up: the card’s message <p> text becomes “Autosi kävi läpi tarjouskilpailun ja tavoitti satojen autoliikkeiden verkoston.”; the note is inserted under the card. Below 768px the illustration is hidden and the gap under the message is 16px instead of production’s 64px.
  • No interaction: nothing in either element is clickable.

The variation’s whole JavaScript: it injects its own CSS, inserts both elements where B2 says, and keeps them in step with the page. Paste it into the VWO variation as written. 1 — The auction result, as an answer

Ready for dev · copy approved by Marketing

Today the block reports two figures and draws a chart of the bidding. The seller’s question is “is this a good offer?”, and two bare numbers answer that only for a reader who already knows how the auction works. The chart is the clearest case: it draws the bids rising, which looks like evidence and is really just the shape every ascending auction makes.

So the chart and the date row go, the figures stay, and the block reads them back as the argument they are. The verdict opens it and the figures close it — evidence reads better after the claim.

One job per layer. The verdict recommends, the middle says what happened, the figures give the scale. The middle used to argue all three at once — 232 characters over six lines on a phone. The payoff line is the headline now and the mechanic is three short sentences beneath it.

Every phrase answers something sellers tell us in research, and none of them are decoration. “perehtyi autoosi” — sellers do not know a person looked at all, and it lands on the car rather than on a document. Not “arvioi”: arvio is what a sceptical seller dismisses (“se on vaan arvio”) and it is also the product’s own word for the machine estimate. “kilpaili siitä” — the dealerships bid against each other to a deadline, the direct answer to “you just pull prices for similar cars”.

Three sentences: who competed, what the competition did to the price, and our view of it. “Kilpailu nosti hinnan 11 500 euroon” ties the figure on the offer card to the auction that produced it, and “Pidämme sitä hyvänä markkinahintana” says why we recommend it, in our own voice. The price is the auction result, the highest offer as the auction ended. A raise the dealership makes later in a negotiation is not something the competition did, so it does not change this figure.

The row stays two cells, and it carries no euro figure. Dealerships, then Korotuksia (raises): every bid in an ascending auction raises the price, which is what the sentence above says the competition did. A third “highest offer” cell was built and dropped: the amount is already the largest thing on the offer card directly above, so the cell restated it in a smaller font and duplicated the price block further down the page. The copy answers the question that cell was really there for.

2 — Tarjous vai pyyntihinta?

Out of scope · not for dev · already being A/B tested in VWO

The concern this block addresses, sellers comparing their offer with Nettiauto prices, is being tested in a different format as front-end tests in VWO. Those tests do not appear in the codebase. This block, and the other price-comparison versions in the prototype (the Nettiauto bar, the side-by-side comparison and the triage), are kept here as design reference only. Do not build them.

The single largest reason a seller rejects, and almost always the same mistake: the number they saw online is a dealership’s advertised price. Nobody was paid that for that car.

The block opens on the claim and then supports it. “Muistathan kun vertaat hintoja, että AutoVex-tarjoukset ja Nettiauton hinnat tarkoittavat eri asioita.” names the two things being confused — work the section title used to do, before it became Tarjous vai pyyntihinta? — which names the confusion rather than the two prices. The second sentence is the correction itself. It stops arguing about the size of the gap and says the advertised number is silent about the seller’s side: “Autoliikkeen pyyntihinta ei kerro, mitä auton edelliselle omistajalle on maksettu.” The reader completes the parallel themselves — that car had a previous owner who was paid something invisible, and they are that person now. Nothing in it defends anyone, so there is nothing for a sceptical reader to bounce off.

Three terms, arrows rather than an equation. An equation asks to be solved; a sequence does not. The middle term names a phase (Jälleenmyynti) rather than a quantity — you cannot subtract a phase the way you can subtract “Kulut”, which is what an earlier version called it.

vastuu is the load-bearing word, and it is an assumption the block invites rather than a claim it makes. It means the dealership’s responsibility for the car after it sells it on. A dealership carrying that liability has reason to put the car right first — so whatever a repair or a set of tyres cost is already inside the advertised price. The block never states this as fact and must not start to.

That is why the footnote no longer lists costs. A list states as fact what is really an assumption about one particular car. The footnote now describes what reselling involves rather than what it costs, hedged with “autosta riippuen”. Do not turn it back into a list.

Only the first term is filled, because it is the only one we hold. The offer is ours to state; the resale phase and the advertised price are not. It wears the “Suosittelemme hyväksymään” tag’s exact green, because the green term is the money the seller gets by accepting. The middle term is dashed, and that is the whole safety mechanism — a dashed box reads as a blank to be filled, not as a quantity, so no proportion is claimed and there is nothing to run backwards. Giving it a figure breaks the block.

Decisions worth keeping

  • It says Autoliikkeen, never ilmoituksen, and that is not a preference. ilmoitus is the seller’s word everywhere else in the product — the funnel submits with “Lähetä ilmoitus”, the success screen says “Ilmoituksesi tarkistetaan”, the preview sheet labels their figure “Pyyntihintasi”. A seller who published an ad twenty minutes ago reads “myynti-ilmoituksen pyyntihinta” as theirs. Naming the dealership closes it, in the lead and in the third term both.
  • The seller’s own term leads, and the chain moves away from it. A diagram that takes costs out of an advertised price says “they took this from you”; one that starts from what the seller is paid says “this is where your number sits”. Same facts, accurate direction, none of the resentment. The euro figure itself came out on 2026-09-28 — the term is named rather than priced, so nothing in the block can go stale against the offer card above it.
  • The middle term never carries a figure. Not even an illustrative one. A euro amount there turns what a dealership adds into what they take off your price, and it restores the arithmetic the dashed box removes.
  • vastuu and riski are the two words doing the work. Refurbishment a sceptical seller will call markup; the risk that the car does not sell, and the liability the dealership carries for it after it does, are real asymmetries they are being relieved of. They are the items that cannot be dismissed as padding — and they are assumptions carried as assumptions, which a list of costs could not be.
  • The figure is the offer as it stands, not the auction result. A negotiation raises the standing amount and the offer card already follows it; this block reads the same single definition, so it cannot quote a stale price beside a live one.
  • No alternative route is named. Not a private sale, not a trade-in, not another dealership. The block answers why that number was bigger and stops.
  • Contrast is measured against the rendered pixels: 8.70:1 for the figure and its sub-label, 14.63:1 for the two dark terms and the arrows, 7.58:1 for the footnote, and 4.76:1 for the slate-500 sub — the lowest, and it passes only because the chip behind it is white.

Open questions from that round

Open questions

  1. Does the seller believe the stack without numbers? It is the central bet. The alternative is an illustrative example car, which buys credibility and costs the two reactions this design avoids: arguing with the arithmetic, and resenting a margin. Worth putting in front of the team before the copy is locked.
  2. The block is gated on the OFFER, and the bidder count is a second guard. It appears only when the offer clears the fair-offer bar — a share of the reference price, which may be a GT-X estimate, a sales advisor’s estimate from the review call, or a later corrected value. buyers >= 4 is checked as well, because a competition argument is false in a thin auction whatever the offer is. That is what lets the headline claim a price rather than a process — and it makes the gate load-bearing: widening the audience is a decision about a claim, not a change to a predicate.
  3. Does a neutral version outperform this one? Both blocks argue for accepting and sit directly above the reject control. A neutral-framing arm is the obvious partner.
  4. What do the other sellers get? Widening past fair offers is the deliberate next step, and the seller with a weaker offer is the one likeliest to reject. Nothing here is designed for them yet.
