Ideation · decision page

Informed decision

A seller looking at an auction result has to choose between accepting, negotiating, rejecting and walking away — and the page gives them nothing to weigh that choice against. This is a first sketch of a section that does: not numbers, but the trade-offs between the routes a seller actually has.

Ideation only · not a build proposal · copy unapproved September 2026

Read this before anything else

  • None of the Finnish on this page is approved copy. The advice section’s wording is AutoVex’s own, revised from an AI first draft; everything in the options section is still that first draft. Neither has been reviewed against the copy guidelines.
  • This is not a build proposal. There is no acceptance criteria section, no data contract and no dev hand-off. It is a switchable sketch, deliberately thin.
  • It deliberately contains no figures. No percentages, no euro gaps, no “x % of sellers”. The brief was to educate rather than to prove, and it also keeps the section clear of any statistic we cannot source.
  • Two of the six ideas make a promise about the dealership — a conditional sale while the seller finds a replacement car, and help with remaining finance. AutoVex does not control either. Wording is hedged to “agree with the dealership”, but OPS has to confirm both are realistic before this goes anywhere near a seller.

Try it

Two new sections above the auction-details block, the auction-details block itself rebuilt, and every Enhanced negotiations change on top. Switchable from the prototype bar under Informed decision (ideation), or by URL:

All three appear only where a decision is still open: the four price tiers, a final offer, and a negotiation the dealership closed. Not on no offers, expired offers, an accepted offer or a rejected one — there the argument is either useless or tactless. Outside those states the auction-details block reverts to what production draws today.

This arm contains Enhanced negotiations too

Turning on Informed decision also turns on every change from the Enhanced negotiations initiative — the card colours and status lines, the rebuilt negotiation modal, the thread, the rounds on the send button, the hero copy, all of it. This is the arm where the whole decision page is being reconsidered, so demoing it against the OLD cards and the OLD modal would be reviewing a page that will never exist.

It is also what makes the colour system legible. Enhanced negotiations establishes green accepts, blue negotiates, red rejects on the buttons; this initiative now carries the same three colours through the new sections. Neither half reads as a system on its own.

The dependency is one-way and Enhanced negotiations is untouched. It is real work in development and stays reviewable by itself: enhanced-negotiations=v1 renders exactly what it always did, with none of the ideation sections. Nothing here changes that initiative, its spec or its scope.

Three blocks, three questions

The arm changes three blocks, and each answers a different question a seller has at this moment. “Muutama neuvo” clears the objection that is stopping them; “Mitä voin tehdä?” lays out the options and what each one costs; and “Tarjouskilpailun tiedot”, the block that already exists, is rebuilt to say whether the offer is any good. They run in that order down the page, which is the order the questions block each other in: the objection stops the seller reading, the options tell them what they can do, and the result tells them whether to.

Three expandable items, styled as the page’s own FAQ blocks. Collapsed, the section is 194 px and the leads alone are the glanceable version; the detail is a tap away, which is what makes it work on a phone. The lead is never a restatement of the concern — an earlier expandable version was rejected for reading as a second FAQ, and what was wrong was the content: four question-and-answer pairs of prose duplicating a component four sections below. Short leads that each answer something on their own are the opposite trade.

The first lead is a question and the other two are statements, deliberately. The asking-price item has to be recognised before it can be answered — a seller who has not seen a higher price elsewhere has no reason to open it. The other two name the conclusion straight out, so a seller who reads only the leads has still been told the useful thing.

The three items

Näitkö korkeamman hinnan muualla?

Autoliikkeen pyyntihinta myytäessä sisältää liikkeen katteen, takuun, kunnostuksen ja riskipreemion. Se ei kerro, mitä autosta lopulta maksetaan myyjälle.

Myös vaihtokaupassa hyvitys voi näyttää korkeammalta kuin se on. Vertaa välirahaa, älä vaihtoautosi hyvityksen summaa.

EN: A dealership’s forecourt price carries their margin, warranty, reconditioning and a risk premium — it does not say what the seller is finally paid. And a trade-in allowance can look higher than it is, so compare the cash difference rather than the allowance.

One item, two paragraphs, and it used to be two items. They are the same misunderstanding wearing two hats: a seller who has seen a bigger number elsewhere has either seen an asking price or been quoted an allowance, and both answers are “that figure is not what anyone pays you”. Split, the seller read two items to learn their number was the same kind of number. The largest single cause of rejection, and the one lead that is a question.

Rahoitusvaje ei ole este.

Voit hyväksyä tarjouksen, vaikka autossa on rahoitusta jäljellä. Jäljellä olevasta rahoituksesta sovitaan autoliikkeen kanssa.

EN: You can accept with a loan outstanding. The remaining finance is agreed with the dealership.

Luovutat auton myöhemmin.

Hyväksymisen jälkeen autoliike ottaa sinuun yhteyttä. Tarkastuksesta, papereista ja luovutuksen ajankohdasta sovitaan autoliikkeen kanssa.

EN: After accepting, the dealership makes contact. Inspection, paperwork and the handover date are agreed with them.

Order is the concerns’ real frequency, with one change: the trade-in item follows the asking-price item rather than sitting last, because both are the same misunderstanding about what a number means.

A note for whoever builds this

The items look like the page’s FAQ blocks but they do not share its open/close. The FAQ pattern does not expand anything — measured on the prototype’s own decision page, the first FAQ answer computes to 0 px tall whether it is open or closed.

The cause: the pattern puts the answer in a sibling of the <details> and animates a grid row from 0fr to 1fr, where the row’s only child has overflow:hidden. An overflow-hidden box has an automatic minimum size of zero, so the track resolves as minmax(0, 1fr) — and with no definite height on the container there is no free space to distribute, so it stays collapsed.

In this section the answer is simply a child of the <details>, so the browser handles it: no CSS mechanism, no JavaScript, nothing to break. The trade-off is no open/close animation — which is what the FAQ delivers today in any case. Production should fix the shared pattern rather than copy this workaround, and the prototype’s own FAQ blocks deserve the same fix.

The trade-in point, in full

This is the one that needed distilling, and the reasoning is deliberately not on the seller’s screen — it is here so the team can check it.

A trade-in figure and a direct-purchase offer are not comparable. A dealership can inflate the allowance on the car being traded in while holding firm on the price of the car the seller actually wants — discounting the new car would lower its perceived value, whereas paying “over the odds” for the old one feels to the seller like beating the system. The two adjustments cancel: the only figure that moves is the cash difference. Consumers largely do not know this, which is why a trade-in quote so often reads as the better deal when it is not.

On screen that is one claim and one line. Anything longer and the item stops being glanceable, which is the whole point of the section.

Nothing is promised on the dealership’s behalf

Every item that touches the deal says the details are agreed with the dealership — which is what accepting actually starts — rather than saying the dealership will accommodate the seller. That distinction is why the loan and handover items need no OPS sign-off, where earlier phrasings did.

One option per button, and they are alternatives

The section is titled “Mitä voin tehdä?”. It used to borrow production’s own “Mitä tapahtuu seuraavaksi?” from the funnel’s next-steps block, so a seller met the same question in the same words at both ends of the journey — and it was the wrong question here. “What happens next” reads as a sequence, so three blocks under it read as three things to do in order, and a seller could reasonably conclude they were meant to accept, then counter-offer, then reject. “What can I do” names a menu, which is what the section is. The funnel’s heading is untouched.

A small “tai” sits between the cards, set into a slate-200 hairline — vertical between columns on a desktop, horizontal between stacked cards on a phone. Three blocks in a row under a heading are a sequence by default; one word fixes what a paragraph of explanation would not.

Three earlier versions all compared AutoVex against a trade-in and a private sale — routes the seller is not choosing between on this page. What is actually in front of them is three buttons: accept, counter-offer, reject. This version says what each one does and what it costs.

A short title carrying the button’s own imperative, then at most three lines. Nothing to expand, no prose, one line per point — and no lead sentence above the cards, because the section title carries the framing on its own.

The lines are reasons to choose, not a description of the state. That is the rule. An earlier pass had the accept card saying the sale closes at the offer price and the dealership will be in touch — both of which the page already says, in the hero and on the card itself, so they cost space and told the seller nothing. Every line now answers “why would I pick this one”.

Hylkää tarjouskilpailu

  • ✕ Menetät kaikki tarjoukset lopullisesti.
  • ✕ Kilpailutus päättyy tähän.

EN: You lose every offer for good · The auction ends here. Marked with an X in a circle in the reject button’s own label colour, so the card and the button read as one action and this reads as the heaviest option. No upside line, and that asymmetry is the message — three crosses would read as preaching.

There is no fourth line about letting the offers expire. One used to sit under the cards — “Jos et tee mitään, tarjoukset umpeutuvat ja kilpailutus päättyy” — on the reasoning that doing nothing is a real choice and the section should be complete. It is gone, for a better reason than the one it replaced: a section headed what happens next that lists four outcomes presents all four as options. Expiry is not an option we offer, it is what happens when the seller does not decide, and naming it beside three buttons quietly makes it a fourth button. The countdown on each offer card already says the deadline exists. Do not restore it for completeness.

The cards are gated on what is actually on screen

The section can never describe a button the seller does not have. The counter-offer card appears only when a counter offer is genuinely possible — the highest offer is not final, the negotiation has not been closed by the dealership, the seller has a round left, and it is not already their turn to wait. The reject card appears only when the reject-all banner is actually rendered.

The auction details, turned into an answer

The third block is not new — it is the existing “Tarjouskilpailun tiedot” section rebuilt. Today it reports two figures and draws a chart of the bidding, and it answers a question the seller is not asking. The question they are asking is “is this a good offer?”, and two bare numbers only answer that for a reader who already knows how a C2B auction works.

The chart is the clearest case of the problem. It shows the bids rising, which looks like evidence and is really just the shape every ascending auction makes. It is true, it is pretty, and it settles nothing.

So the numbers stay and the chart and date row go. In their place, the block reads those same figures back as the argument they are: professionals at the dealerships reviewed the listing and then bid against each other, so the top bid is an expert field’s competitive result rather than one buyer’s opinion. Then AutoVex states a view in its own voice and recommends accepting — which is the sentence the seller is looking for, and the one nothing on this page says today.

An earlier draft argued from blind independent valuation instead — “each dealership priced the car itself and did not see the other offers”. Replaced deliberately: that is a mechanic a seller has to be taught before it means anything, where “professionals looked at your ad and competed for it” lands on first reading. The blindness is still true; it just does not carry the argument for a reader who has never run an auction.

Hyvä tarjouskilpailu

Pidämme korkeinta tarjousta kilpailukykyisenä hintana autostasi ja suosittelemme hyväksymään sen.

EN — badge: A good auction. Headline: 25 dealerships competed for your car. Body: Professionals at the dealerships reviewed your listing and competed against each other for your car. So the highest offer is not one dealership’s estimate, it is the result of 25 dealerships competing. Closing line: We consider the highest offer a competitive price for your car, and we recommend accepting it.

Three parts, and each is doing one job

  • The headline is the fact, in the seller’s own terms. Not “25 bidders” as a count but 25 businesses competing for their specific car. It is the same figure the row above already shows, said as a sentence.
  • The body is the mechanic, and it is the only part that actually does the work. Two claims in one sentence: the bidders are professionals who looked at the listing, and they bid against each other. Without them, 25 is just a big number and a seller who distrusts the offer has no reason to read it as anything else.
  • The closing block is a judgement, and it is deliberately AutoVex speaking. The rest of the block lets the seller draw the conclusion; this says it out loud, and it is the only place on the decision page where the service states a view on the offer rather than reporting it — and the only place that recommends an action outright. It names no euro figure: korkeinta tarjousta points at the card above, so it cannot go stale against the number beside it and the sentence works unchanged on a final offer.

Presentation borrows the warm-up screen — illustration, headline, one paragraph — at the scale of a block inside a card. It is the warm-up’s own illustration, so a seller who came through that screen meets it twice, two screens apart. Deliberate: this section is the explanation the warm-up’s “Hyvä tarjouskilpailu takana!” never gave.

“Hyvä tarjouskilpailu” used to be a separate lime badge above the headline, with the recommendation in a blue strip below. Two green-ish elements saying the same thing at two lengths, in two borrowed colours — lime from the warm-up badge, where it reports a status, and blue from the options section’s promoted card. They are one green block now: the badge is its lead line, the sentence is the explanation. That also lets the column open on the headline, which gives the section a clean arc — the fact, then why it means anything, then the conclusion.

No rule between the stats row and the illustration. The stats grid carries its own frame, so a hairline 20 px under it read as a second line boxing the illustration in rather than as a separator. Spacing does the separating.

Where it deliberately does not appear

Two gates, and in both the block falls back to today’s design with nothing removed — the chart and the date row exactly as they are now.

Gate Why
Fewer than four bidding dealershipsThe argument is simply false. A thin auction is its own state and needs its own version of this block — one that does not yet exist — and showing this one there would be the worst available answer to “is this a good offer?”
No decision left to makeThe same open-decision rule the other two sections use. After a rejection, “we consider that a competitive price” is the service telling a seller who has walked away that they were wrong; there is nothing left for it to help with, and it costs trust to make the point.

Worth knowing before reading the first gate as theoretical. A benchmarking study of comparable services put an uncompetitive auction — fewer than four bidding dealerships — behind roughly half of all rejected-offer cases. It is not an edge case; it is the second half of this design, and it is not designed.

Choices made, and why

Two marks, not three. A green tick for an upside, a red X for a cost. A slate dash for “a trade-off, not an alarm” was the third and has been retired: the counter offer’s time cost now carries the same cross the reject card does, so the only distinction left is upside or cost. The cross is an X in a circle in the reject button’s own label colour, which is why the reject card and the button read as one action. Spent in two places rather than one it no longer marks the heaviest option on its own — the reject card earns that by having no upside line at all. It still does not collide with red-means-unread, because it sits inside a card whose title names an action rather than in a status line. The tick carries the action’s colour and the cross carries severity. Green ticks the accept card, blue ticks the counter-offer card — the same blue as the counter-offer button — so an upside is written in the colour of the action it belongs to, while the cross stays red wherever it appears because it always means the same thing: this costs you something. With only one card painted, the marks are where the per-action colour system actually lives in this section. That is why the counter-offer card carries a blue tick and a red cross and is not inconsistent.

Two claims were removed on purpose, and both would have been wrong. Nothing says the price is now fixed — a dealership can still adjust after inspecting the car, so that was a promise we cannot keep, and it is not replaced with a hedge either. And nothing calls a counter offer binding: it closes the sale mechanically when a dealership accepts, but it is not legally binding, and saying so would misstate what the seller is agreeing to.

No column is painted, and the hierarchy is in the titles. Accept is bold in dark green; the two alternatives are medium weight in a lighter slate. Accept leads by being the darkest and heaviest thing in the row while the others recede — not by being boxed.

That took three passes, and the route is worth keeping. Accept was originally blue-filled because it was the promoted one, which taught blue as “the recommended action” on a page where blue already means “negotiate”. Tinting all three in their own buttons’ colours fixed the meaning and cost the hierarchy — three painted cards is three promoted cards. Painting accept alone was right on both counts and still louder than a section of quiet advice wants to be: a filled, outlined box beside two bare columns reads as a card next to not-cards.

The colour system is carried by the marks and the real buttons instead, which is enough. Do not re-introduce a fill or an outline on any of the three, and do not take accept back to blue. Nothing in any column overstates: every line is a verified production consequence.

Nothing quantitative is shown. The brief was to educate rather than prove.

Open questions

  1. The verdict is triggered by the bidder count, not by the offer — and it now recommends accepting. “We consider this a competitive price, and we recommend accepting it” appears whenever four or more dealerships bid. But a well-attended auction can still land below what we would ourselves estimate the car is worth, and there that sentence is wrong in the one direction that costs the most trust: the seller takes our advice and later finds out. The recommendation raises the stakes on this — stating a view is one thing, telling someone what to do is another. The honest shape is probably the reverse of what is built: gate the claim on the offer measured against our own estimate, and let the bidder count be the evidence for it rather than the trigger. That needs the estimate on the page, which is a product decision this sketch does not make.
  2. The thin-auction version does not exist. Fewer than four bidders falls back to today’s chart, which is the safe answer and not a good one: that seller has the same question and a worse offer, and is the likeliest of all to reject. What the block should say there is the harder half of this design and it is untouched.
  3. The section keeps production’s title. “Tarjouskilpailun tiedot” — auction details — described a record of what happened. The block is now an argument about what it meant, so the title undersells it. Left alone rather than changed in passing, because renaming a production section is a decision of its own.
  4. The price anchor is no longer addressed anywhere. Earlier versions opened with a line separating a listing’s asking price from an offer — the largest single cause of rejection. It does not belong in a section about what the buttons do. If it is worth saying, it needs its own place on the page rather than being folded back in here.
  5. Net proceeds after loan payoff. A benchmarking study of comparable services found no competitor that shows a live payoff figure at offer time — all of them make the seller fetch a payoff letter first. An estimate of offer minus remaining loan, before the seller spends time on a review call, would lead the category. Needs data and a product decision.
  6. Conditional acceptance, borrowed from property sale-contingency clauses: “I accept, contingent on my replacement completing by date X”. No car service found running it, and it is the honest answer for a seller who has not found a replacement — a concern this version has no room for at all.
  7. Does a neutral version outperform this one? Only the accept card is promoted, the auction-details block now tells the seller outright to accept, and all of it sits above a reject button. The prototype’s own anti-pattern notes warn about exactly this shape. Explaining is a gentler mechanism than scoring, but a neutral-framing arm is the obvious A/B partner and it has got more necessary, not less.
  8. Should the reject card carry an upside? It has none today. If sellers can straightforwardly start a new auction later, saying so would be honest and would stop the card reading as a warning.
